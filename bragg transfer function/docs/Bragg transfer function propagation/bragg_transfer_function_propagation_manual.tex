\begin{abstract}
This manual documents both propagation models implemented in the propagation-aware Python calculator. In a single-frequency retroreflected geometry, the forward optical envelope and its reflected copy reach the atoms with the round-trip separation $\taud=2L/c$. Their overlap determines the effective two-photon Rabi envelope. Model A rescales each overlap to restore the nominal $\pi/2$--$\pi$--$\pi/2$ areas. Model B holds the input Rabi rates fixed, retains the overlap-induced area error and normalizes the phase-step response by the actual output-fringe slope. The manual derives the square and Gaussian overlap, explains the sensitivity and transfer functions, and gives separate Model-A and Model-B examples at $L=0$, \SI{150}{\metre} and \SI{2000}{\metre}. All examples use $T=\SI{250}{\milli\second}$, $\tau_{\pi}=\SI{50}{\micro\second}$ and $\tau_{\pi/2}=\SI{25}{\micro\second}$. For Gaussian pulses, these widths are intensity FWHM values.
\end{abstract}

\begin{keybox}[Main result]
Model A isolates pulse-shape changes after area recalibration: a delayed square pulse becomes shorter and higher, while an untruncated compensated Gaussian retains its original magnitude response. Model B instead exposes the loss of pulse area at fixed input power. At long distance this loss can strongly reduce the output-fringe slope, especially for the square pulse, making the phase-normalized transfer function large and experimentally ill-conditioned. In both models, pulse shaping modifies $\Hatom$ but does not remove the common propagation factor $D_d$.
\end{keybox}

\section{Purpose and model boundary}

The calculator describes a resonant three-pulse Mach--Zehnder sequence with nominal areas $\pi/2$--$\pi$--$\pi/2$. It extends the finite-pulse sensitivity-function treatment of Cheinet \textit{et al.}\cite{Cheinet2008}, the MIGA phase-noise analysis\cite{Canuel2018}, and the pulse-shaping description based on accumulated Rabi area\cite{Fang2018}. This manual covers both interface settings: \texttt{A: area compensated} and \texttt{B: fixed input}.

The backend is an effective resonant two-level model. The Bragg order $n$ multiplies the phase response but does not alter the normalized pulse dynamics. The calculation includes delayed envelope overlap, pulse-area recalibration, finite-pulse filtering and laser phase/frequency-noise conversion. It does not include the momentum-state ladder, velocity selectivity, diffraction phase, AC Stark shifts, Doppler detuning, spontaneous emission, parasitic interferometers or cavity dynamics. These effects require a multi-state Bragg treatment such as Refs.~\cite{Siemss2020,Jenewein2022}.

\section{Geometry and delayed two-photon coupling}

Let $I(t)$ and $\phi(t)$ denote the source-side intensity envelope and optical phase. Taking the forward field at the atoms as the time reference, the reflected field samples the source a round-trip time earlier,
\begin{equation}
  \taud=\frac{2L}{c}.
  \label{eq:delay}
\end{equation}
After removing constant propagation phases and assuming identical spatial modes, the complex two-photon coupling is
\begin{equation}
 \widetilde{\Omega}(t)=\Omega_{\mathrm{in}}
 \frac{\sqrt{I(t)I(t-\taud)}}{I_{\mathrm{pk}}}
 \exp\!\left\{\ii[\phi(t)-\phi(t-\taud)]\right\}.
 \label{eq:complex-rabi}
\end{equation}
Its modulus gives the raw envelope
\begin{equation}
 \Oraw(t)=\Omega_{\mathrm{in}}
 \frac{\sqrt{I(t)I(t-\taud)}}{I_{\mathrm{pk}}},
 \label{eq:raw-rabi}
\end{equation}
while its argument gives the local counter-propagating phase. Envelope overlap modifies the pulse shape and therefore $\Hatom$. Phase differencing produces the separate delay filter $D_d$ derived in \cref{sec:transfer}. The two effects enter at different stages of the calculation.

\begin{warningbox}[Definition of $L$]
The software uses $\taud=2L/c$, where $L$ is one way. Entering a path length that already includes the round trip counts the factor of two twice. Additional fibre, electronic or cavity delays are not included automatically.
\end{warningbox}

\section{Square and Gaussian overlap}

\subsection{Square pulse}

For a unit-height square command of duration $\tau$ centred on $t_c$, the common interval has duration
\begin{equation}
 \tau_{\mathrm{ov}}=\tau-\taud,
 \qquad \tau>\taud,
 \label{eq:square-overlap}
\end{equation}
and is centred at $t_c+\taud/2$. The raw rate is $\Omega_{\mathrm{in}}$ inside that interval, so the area ratio is
\begin{equation}
 \frac{A_{\mathrm{raw}}}{A_0}=1-\frac{\taud}{\tau}.
 \label{eq:square-area-ratio}
\end{equation}
If $\taud\geq\tau$, there is no common support and the software stops with an error.

\subsection{Gaussian pulse}

The Gaussian input convention is
\begin{equation}
 I(t)=I_{\mathrm{pk}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{(t-t_c)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right],
 \qquad \tau_{\mathrm{FWHM}}=2\sqrt{2\ln2}\,\sigma.
 \label{eq:gaussian-input}
\end{equation}
Substitution into \cref{eq:raw-rabi} gives
\begin{equation}
 \Oraw(t)=\Omega_{\mathrm{in}}
 \exp\!\left[-\frac{\taud^2}{8\sigma^2}\right]
 \exp\!\left[-\frac{(t-t_c-\taud/2)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right].
 \label{eq:gaussian-overlap}
\end{equation}
The overlap is therefore a Gaussian of the same width, shifted by $\taud/2$. Its peak attenuation is the first exponential in \cref{eq:gaussian-overlap}. The calculation truncates each input envelope at $\pm6\sigma$. The common numerical support then has total duration $12\sigma-\taud$, and its small missing-tail correction is retained through the error function.

\section{Model A: pulse-area restoration}

For every interaction $j$, Model A defines
\begin{equation}
 \alpha_j=\frac{A_j^{\mathrm{target}}}{A_j^{\mathrm{raw}}},
 \qquad
 {\Oeff}_j(t)=\alpha_j{\Oraw}_j(t),
 \qquad
 A_j^{\mathrm{target}}\in\{\pi/2,\pi,\pi/2\}.
 \label{eq:model-a}
\end{equation}
It represents an experimental recalibration of the local pulse area after propagation has been included.

For a square pulse, \cref{eq:square-area-ratio} gives
\begin{equation}
 \alpha_{\mathrm{sq}}=\frac{\tau}{\tau-\taud},
 \qquad
 \Omega_{\mathrm{eff,pk}}=\Omega_{\mathrm{in}}\alpha_{\mathrm{sq}}.
 \label{eq:square-compensation}
\end{equation}
The compensated pulse is shorter and higher than the no-delay pulse, so its Fourier envelope changes.

For an untruncated Gaussian, the area attenuation is exactly the peak attenuation. Hence
\begin{equation}
 \alpha_{\mathrm{G}}=\exp\!\left[\frac{\taud^2}{8\sigma^2}\right],
 \qquad
 {\Oeff}_{\mathrm{G}}(t)=\Omega_{\mathrm{in}}
 \exp\!\left[-\frac{(t-t_c-\taud/2)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right].
 \label{eq:gaussian-compensation}
\end{equation}
Thus Model A restores the original Gaussian exactly, except for the common time shift. In the implemented $\pm6\sigma$ representation, the normalization also includes the ratio of the nominal and common-support error functions. Because the removed tails are extremely small in the present example, the numerical result is indistinguishable from \cref{eq:gaussian-compensation} at the plotted precision.

\section{Model B: fixed input Rabi rates}

Model B applies no post-overlap power correction:
\begin{equation}
 \alpha_j=1,
 \qquad
 {\Oeff}_j(t)={\Oraw}_j(t),
 \qquad
 A_j^{\mathrm{eff}}=A_j^{\mathrm{raw}}.
 \label{eq:model-b}
\end{equation}
For a square pulse, the remaining fraction of the target area is
\begin{equation}
 \frac{A_{\mathrm{B,sq}}}{A_{\mathrm{target}}}
 =1-\frac{\taud}{\tau}.
 \label{eq:model-b-square}
\end{equation}
For a Gaussian pulse with the implemented truncated support, it is
\begin{equation}
 \frac{A_{\mathrm{B,G}}}{A_{\mathrm{target}}}
 =\exp\!\left[-\frac{\taud^2}{8\sigma^2}\right]
 \frac{\operatorname{erf}\!\left[(6\sigma-\taud/2)/(\sqrt{2}\sigma)\right]}
 {\operatorname{erf}(6/\sqrt{2})}.
 \label{eq:model-b-gaussian}
\end{equation}
Thus Model B represents an experiment in which the programmed duration and input optical power remain fixed as $L$ changes. The effective pulse areas cease to be exactly $\pi/2$ and $\pi$, so the ideal plateau formula for $s(t)$ is no longer generally valid.

\section{Sensitivity function}

Define the accumulated area of interaction $j$ as
\begin{equation}
 \Theta_j(t)=\int_{t_{j,\mathrm{s}}}^{t}{\Oeff}_j(t')\dd t'.
\end{equation}
Because Model A restores ideal areas, the MIGA/Cheinet sign convention gives
\begin{equation}
s(t)=
\begin{cases}
 \sin\Theta_1(t), & t\text{ in the first }\pi/2\text{ pulse},\\
 +1, & \text{between pulses 1 and 2},\\
 \cos\Theta_2(t), & t\text{ in the }\pi\text{ pulse},\\
 -1, & \text{between pulses 2 and 3},\\
 -\cos\Theta_3(t), & t\text{ in the last }\pi/2\text{ pulse},\\
 0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:sensitivity}
\end{equation}
The program evaluates the equivalent resonant two-level phase-step response. The two constructions agree for the restored $\pi/2$--$\pi$--$\pi/2$ areas.

For Model B, the program instead propagates the two-level state through the three actual pulse rotations. If $P$ is the output transition probability, the phase sensitivity is obtained numerically from
\begin{equation}
 s_{\mathrm B}(t)
 =-\frac{\partial P/\partial\delta\phi(t)}{\partial P/\partial\Phi_{\mathrm{out}}},
 \label{eq:model-b-sensitivity}
\end{equation}
where $\delta\phi(t)$ is an infinitesimal phase step applied at time $t$, and $\Phi_{\mathrm{out}}$ is the scanned output phase at the nominal mid-fringe point. This normalization expresses the result as inferred atom phase. If the denominator becomes small, the inferred phase response becomes large and noise-sensitive. Such a result indicates poor fringe readout, not enhanced interferometer performance.

\section{Transfer functions}\label{sec:transfer}

Using
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal{F}[x](\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)e^{-\ii\omega t}\dd t,
 \qquad \omega=2\pi f,
\end{equation}
the atomic response to local Bragg phase is
\begin{equation}
 \Hatom(f)=n\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dot{s}(t)e^{-\ii2\pi ft}\dd t
 =n\,\ii2\pi f\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}s(t)e^{-\ii2\pi ft}\dd t.
 \label{eq:hai}
\end{equation}
The second form follows by integration by parts because $s(t)$ vanishes outside the sequence. The code uses this second, numerically stable form. The delay between the two source-phase samples gives
\begin{equation}
 D_d(f)=1-e^{-\ii2\pi f\taud},
 \qquad |D_d|=2|\sin(\pi f\taud)|.
 \label{eq:delay-filter}
\end{equation}
The source-phase and source-frequency responses are
\begin{equation}
 \Hsrc(f)=\Hatom(f)D_d(f),
 \qquad
 \Hnu(f)=\frac{\Hsrc(f)}{\ii f}.
 \label{eq:combined}
\end{equation}
The factor $2\pi$ cancels when frequency noise is specified in hertz because $\dot{\phi}=2\pi\delta\nu$.

\end{equation}
An amplitude spectral density is multiplied by $|H_x|$, not by $|H_x|^2$.

\section{Numerical implementation}

The schedule builder converts entered widths to nominal peak Rabi rates. For square pulses, $\Omega_{\pi/2}=(\pi/2)/\tau_{\pi/2}$ and $\Omega_\pi=\pi/\tau_\pi$. For Gaussian pulses, the target area is divided by $\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}\operatorname{erf}(6/\sqrt{2})$. The delayed overlap and raw area are calculated separately for each interaction. Model A then applies the area scale; Model B leaves the scale equal to one.

The transfer routine evaluates $\int s(t)e^{-\ii\omega t}\dd t$. It uses Gauss--Legendre quadrature inside the pulses and integrates the long constant plateaus analytically with
\begin{equation}
 \int_0^d e^{\ii qt}\dd t
 =d\,e^{\ii qd/2}\operatorname{sinc}\!\left(\frac{qd}{2}\right),
 \qquad \operatorname{sinc}(x)=\frac{\sin x}{x}.
\end{equation}
NumPy uses $\operatorname{sinc}(x)=\sin(\pi x)/(\pi x)$, so the implementation passes $qd/(2\pi)$. Frequencies are processed in chunks and the quadrature order grows with the product of maximum frequency and pulse support.

\begin{checkbox}[Validation]
The built-in self-test verifies the zero-delay limit, the square overlap formula, Model-A area restoration, Model-B raw-area retention and finite fringe slope, the analytic delay factor, $\Hsrc=\Hatom D_d$, and $\Hnu=\Hsrc/(\ii f)$ away from DC.
\end{checkbox}

\section{Model-A worked comparison: $L=0$, \SI{150}{\metre} and \SI{2000}{\metre}}\label{sec:case}

\subsection{Common inputs}

The three examples use the same $n=1$, $T=\SI{250}{\milli\second}$, \SI{25}{\micro\second} $\pi/2$ width and \SI{50}{\micro\second} $\pi$ width. For square pulses, these are command durations and both nominal rates are $\Omega/(2\pi)=\SI{10}{\kilo\hertz}$. For Gaussian pulses, they are intensity FWHM values, giving $\sigma_{\pi/2}=\SI{\CaseGaussHalfSigmaUs}{\micro\second}$, $\sigma_{\pi}=\SI{\CaseGaussPiSigmaUs}{\micro\second}$ and the common area-normalized input peak $\Omega_0/(2\pi)=\SI{\CaseGaussInputPeak}{\kilo\hertz}$. The frequency range is \SI{0.1}{\hertz}--\SI{100}{\kilo\hertz}.

The round-trip delays are
\begin{equation}
 \taud(L=0)=0,\qquad
 \taud(\SI{150}{\metre})=\SI{\CaseDelayOneFiftyUs}{\micro\second},\qquad
 \taud(\SI{2000}{\metre})=\SI{\CaseDelayTwoThousandUs}{\micro\second}.
\end{equation}
$L=0$ is therefore the no-propagation reference, whereas \SI{2000}{\metre} is a deliberately demanding case in which the delay is no longer small relative to the \SI{25}{\micro\second} pulse.

\subsection{Atomic transfer function}

At frequencies of order $1/T$, all three distances and both shapes are almost indistinguishable because the long free evolution dominates the microsecond pulse edges. At higher frequency, the envelope depends strongly on pulse shape. The square response develops sinc-like lobes and retains more high-frequency content. The smooth Gaussian response decays much more rapidly.

For the propagated square pulse, the shorter compensated duration shifts the high-frequency lobes relative to the no-delay calculation. For the Gaussian pulse, \cref{eq:gaussian-compensation} implies
\begin{equation}
 H_{\mathrm{AI,G}}^{(L)}(f)
 =e^{-\ii2\pi f\taud/2}H_{\mathrm{AI,G}}^{(0)}(f)
\end{equation}
in the untruncated limit. The propagation changes its complex phase but not its magnitude.

The Gaussian values remain equal to the displayed precision: under Model A, distance contributes a time shift to the effective Gaussian and therefore a phase factor to $\Hatom$, but no magnitude penalty. Its much smaller \SI{100}{\kilo\hertz} value is smooth pulse-shape filtering, not an extra propagation loss.

\begin{keybox}[Interpretation of the three-distance comparison]
$L=0$ isolates the ordinary atomic transfer function but has no differential propagation coupling. At \SI{150}{\metre}, square-pulse distortion and area compensation are small, while the delay factor is already nonzero. At \SI{2000}{\metre}, square overlap is severely shortened, the required $\pi/2$ Rabi rate more than doubles, and the delay-filter zero enters the plotted band. Gaussian Model-A compensation still restores $|\Hatom|$, but both pulse shapes retain the same unavoidable delayed sampling of source phase through $D_d$.
\end{keybox}

\section{Model-B worked comparison: fixed input Rabi rates}\label{sec:model-b-case}

Model B repeats exactly the same $n$, $T$, pulse-width and distance inputs as the Model-A example, but applies no area scale. The square effective peak therefore remains \SI{10}{\kilo\hertz}; the Gaussian effective peak is reduced by the overlap attenuation in \cref{eq:model-b-gaussian}. The common supports and delay factor are unchanged from Model A because they are geometric properties of the envelope overlap.

\subsection{Model-B atomic response}

Because the pulse areas are nonideal, propagation now changes both the shape of $s(t)$ and the readout slope used in \cref{eq:model-b-sensitivity}. The \SI{150}{\metre} curves remain close to $L=0$. At \SI{2000}{\metre}, the square response is strongly amplified after phase normalization because the denominator is close to zero. This amplified $|\Hatom|$ must be interpreted together with the lost fringe slope; it is not an improvement in signal-to-noise ratio.

\subsection{Model-B total phase-noise response}

The factor $D_d$ is exactly the same as in \cref{tab:case-delay}; changing the power-control model cannot change propagation time. Model B changes the total response only through its different $\Hatom$. The long-baseline Gaussian therefore gives a much smaller high-frequency total response than the square pulse in this fixed-input example, while the low-frequency coupling remains governed by both the delay and the nonideal readout normalization.

\label{fig:model-b-total}

\begin{warningbox}[Model-B long-baseline square case]
For $L=\SI{2000}{\metre}$ and the specified \SI{25}{\micro\second}/\SI{50}{\micro\second} square pulses, the phase-normalized response is close to an ill-conditioned operating point. A realistic performance estimate must propagate detection noise and contrast through the actual fringe slope, and should include detuning, diffraction phase and multi-momentum-order dynamics before treating this case as experimentally viable.
\end{warningbox}

\section{Troubleshooting and limitations}

\begin{longtable}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.27\textwidth}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.67\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
Observation & Explanation and action \\
\midrule
\endhead
No square-pulse overlap & $\taud\geq\tau$ for at least one pulse. Reduce $L$ or lengthen the pulse. \\
Gaussian support looks much longer than its entered width & The entered width is FWHM; the numerical support retains the tails out to $\pm6\sigma$. \\
Gaussian propagation curves overlap exactly & This is the expected Model-A result in \cref{eq:gaussian-compensation}, not a missing calculation. Inspect complex phase to observe the $\taud/2$ shift. \\
Square relative-change spikes & The no-delay response is near a lobe zero. Compare complex responses or a local envelope rather than a pointwise ratio. \\
$|\Hatom|$ becomes very large in Model B & Check the reported output-fringe slope. Phase normalization divides by this slope; a small value signals an ill-conditioned readout rather than improved sensitivity. \\
$\Hatom$ is dimensionless but $\Hnu$ is rad/Hz & They have different inputs: phase/phase versus phase/frequency. \\
Required square peak is too high & The area-restoring factor grows as $\tau/(\tau-\taud)$. The assumed compensation may exceed available optical power. \\
Result differs from a multi-order simulation & The present backend is an effective resonant two-level model and excludes unwanted momentum orders and diffraction phases. \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}
